## Emergency warning signs — call 911 or go to the ER

Some pregnancy and postpartum symptoms need emergency care right away, not a wait-and-see.
Go to the ER or call 911 for: chest pain or pressure; trouble breathing or shortness of
breath at rest; a severe headache that won't go away or is the worst you've ever had;
changes in vision such as blurriness, flashing lights, or blind spots; heavy vaginal
bleeding (soaking a pad in an hour); a seizure; thoughts of harming yourself or your baby;
fainting; or a fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher with feeling very unwell. Trust your
instincts — if something feels seriously wrong, seek care immediately. It is always okay
to get checked out.

## Preeclampsia — what it is and its warning signs

Preeclampsia is a serious blood-pressure condition that usually appears after 20 weeks of
pregnancy and can also occur in the weeks after delivery. It involves high blood pressure
plus signs that organs are under stress. Warning signs include a severe or persistent
headache, vision changes (blurriness, flashing lights, spots), pain in the upper-right
belly or under the ribs, sudden swelling of the face or hands, nausea or vomiting in the
second half of pregnancy, and sudden weight gain. Preeclampsia can be dangerous even when
you feel okay, which is why blood-pressure monitoring matters. If you notice these signs,
contact your provider right away; severe headache or vision changes warrant emergency care.

## High blood pressure in pregnancy and reading your numbers

Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic (for example, 118/75). In pregnancy,
a reading at or above 140/90 on two occasions is considered high, and 160/110 or above is
severe and needs urgent attention. To measure accurately, sit with your back supported and
feet flat, rest for five minutes first, keep your arm at heart level, and don't talk during
the reading. Take it at the same times each day when you can. A single high reading isn't a
diagnosis, but repeated high readings — or any severe reading — should be reported to your
provider promptly because high blood pressure is the main sign of preeclampsia.

## Swelling (edema) — normal versus concerning

Mild swelling of the feet, ankles, and lower legs is common and usually normal in
pregnancy, especially late in the day, in hot weather, or after standing a long time. It
often improves with rest, elevating your feet, staying hydrated, and moving around.
Swelling becomes concerning when it is sudden, when it affects your face or hands or around
the eyes, or when it comes with a bad headache, vision changes, or upper-belly pain — those
can be signs of preeclampsia and should be reported to your provider promptly. Swelling in
just one leg, especially with pain, warmth, or redness in the calf, can signal a blood clot
and needs same-day medical evaluation.

## Headaches in pregnancy

Headaches are common in pregnancy, often from hormones, dehydration, lack of sleep, stress,
or caffeine changes, and many are not dangerous. Helpful steps include drinking water,
resting in a dark quiet room, eating regularly, and asking your provider which pain
relievers are safe for you. A headache is a red flag when it is sudden and severe, the worst
you've ever had, doesn't ease with rest or usual measures, or comes with vision changes,
upper-belly pain, or swelling of the face and hands — especially after 20 weeks. Those can
point to preeclampsia and should prompt an urgent call to your provider or emergency care.

## Fetal movement and counting kicks

Feeling your baby move is one of the most reassuring signs of well-being. Most people start
to feel movement between 18 and 25 weeks. By the third trimester, babies tend to have their
own pattern of active and quiet times. A common way to check is "count the kicks": at a time
your baby is usually active, sit or lie quietly and note how long it takes to feel 10
movements; many babies reach 10 within two hours. The most important thing is knowing your
baby's normal pattern. If movements clearly slow down, change a lot, or stop, contact your
provider or labor and delivery right away — do not wait until the next day.

## Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes is high blood sugar that develops during pregnancy, usually screened
for around 24 to 28 weeks with a glucose test. It often has no symptoms, which is why
screening matters. It's managed with balanced meals (pairing carbohydrates with protein,
spreading carbs across the day), regular gentle activity, monitoring blood sugar if your
provider asks, and sometimes medication or insulin. Well-managed gestational diabetes
usually leads to healthy outcomes. Left unmanaged it can raise the chance of a larger baby,
high blood pressure, and delivery complications. Report very high or very low readings, or
symptoms like extreme thirst, frequent urination, or shakiness, to your care team.

## Normal heart changes in pregnancy

Pregnancy puts extra demand on the heart: blood volume rises by roughly 40 to 50 percent,
the resting heart rate goes up by about 10 to 20 beats per minute, and the heart pumps more
blood each minute. As a result, feeling your heart beat faster, mild breathlessness when
active, and occasional lightheadedness can be normal. What is not expected and should be
checked: chest pain, breathlessness at rest or when lying flat, fainting, a racing or
irregular heartbeat that doesn't settle, or breathlessness that wakes you at night. These
can signal a heart-rhythm or heart-function problem and deserve prompt medical evaluation.

## Heart palpitations during pregnancy

Palpitations — a sensation that your heart is racing, pounding, fluttering, or skipping —
are common in pregnancy because of higher blood volume and hormonal changes, and most are
harmless. Triggers include caffeine, dehydration, anxiety, low blood sugar, anemia, and
thyroid changes. Steps that help are staying hydrated, limiting caffeine, eating regularly,
and rest. Seek prompt care if palpitations come with chest pain, severe shortness of breath,
fainting or near-fainting, or if a rapid or irregular heartbeat lasts and won't settle on
its own. Frequent or sustained palpitations are worth mentioning to your provider so they
can check for anemia, thyroid issues, or a rhythm problem.

## Peripartum cardiomyopathy

Peripartum cardiomyopathy is a rare but serious weakening of the heart muscle that can occur
in late pregnancy or in the months after delivery, reducing how well the heart pumps. Its
symptoms overlap with normal pregnancy, which is why they're easy to miss: shortness of
breath when lying flat or that wakes you at night, a persistent cough, swelling in the legs
or feet, fast weight gain from fluid, fatigue, a racing or pounding heartbeat, and chest
discomfort. Because it can worsen quickly, these symptoms — especially breathlessness at
rest or when lying down — should be reported to a provider promptly, and severe shortness of
breath or chest pain is an emergency.

## Vaginal bleeding in pregnancy

Light spotting can happen in early pregnancy and is often harmless, but bleeding should
always be mentioned to your provider so they can advise you. Heavy bleeding (soaking a pad
in an hour), bleeding with cramping or belly pain, or passing clots or tissue needs urgent
evaluation. In the second half of pregnancy, any vaginal bleeding should be treated as
urgent and checked the same day, because it can signal a problem with the placenta. When in
doubt, call your provider or labor and delivery — describing how much, what color, and
whether there's pain helps them guide you.

## Signs of preterm labor

Labor before 37 weeks is called preterm. Warning signs include regular tightening or
contractions (more than a few in an hour), a low dull backache that comes and goes, pelvic
pressure that feels like the baby is pushing down, menstrual-like cramps, a change or
increase in vaginal discharge, or a gush or trickle of fluid that could be your water
breaking. If you notice these before 37 weeks, contact your provider or labor and delivery
right away — early evaluation can sometimes slow or stop preterm labor and protect the baby.
Drinking water and resting on your side while you call can help while you await guidance.

## Nausea, vomiting, and hyperemesis gravidarum

Nausea and vomiting ("morning sickness") are very common in the first trimester and usually
ease by around weeks 12 to 16. Eating small frequent meals, dry bland foods like crackers,
ginger, staying hydrated, and avoiding triggers can help. It becomes a concern — called
hyperemesis gravidarum — when vomiting is severe and constant, you can't keep fluids down,
you have signs of dehydration (very dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth, little urination), or
you're losing weight. That level of vomiting needs medical care because you may need fluids
and anti-nausea treatment; contact your provider rather than trying to push through it.

## Postpartum hemorrhage

Postpartum hemorrhage is heavy bleeding after birth and is a medical emergency. After
delivery some bleeding (lochia) is expected and gradually lightens over days to weeks.
Warning signs that need immediate care include soaking through one pad or more every hour,
passing clots larger than a golf ball, bleeding that suddenly gets heavier instead of
lighter, feeling faint, dizzy, or weak, a racing heartbeat, or pale clammy skin. These can
happen in the first day or weeks after birth. If you have heavy postpartum bleeding with any
of these signs, call 911 or go to the ER — do not wait to see if it slows down.

## Postpartum preeclampsia

Preeclampsia can develop or continue after delivery, usually within the first few days to
six weeks postpartum, and it's easy to dismiss because the focus has shifted to the baby.
Warning signs are the same as in pregnancy: a severe or persistent headache, vision changes,
upper-right belly pain, sudden swelling of the face or hands, shortness of breath, and high
blood pressure. Because it can lead to seizures or stroke if untreated, these symptoms after
birth should be treated as urgent — contact your provider right away, and severe headache,
vision changes, or trouble breathing warrant emergency care. New parents should keep
watching their blood pressure even after going home.

## Postpartum depression and anxiety

Many people feel weepy, anxious, or overwhelmed in the first week or two after birth — the
"baby blues" — which usually lift on their own. It may be postpartum depression or anxiety
when low mood, hopelessness, constant worry, irritability, trouble bonding with the baby,
sleep or appetite changes, or feeling like a failure last more than two weeks or feel
intense. This is common and treatable, and it is not a personal failing. Reach out to your
provider about how you're feeling — support, therapy, and sometimes medication help. Seek
help immediately if you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or thoughts that
your family would be better off without you.

## When to call your provider versus go to the ER

A useful rule of thumb: call your provider (same day) for things that are new, persistent,
or worrying but not life-threatening — for example, a blood pressure that's mildly high,
swelling that's increasing, ongoing headaches, reduced but still present fetal movement, or
mood changes lasting more than two weeks. Go to the ER or call 911 for emergencies — chest
pain, trouble breathing at rest, the worst headache of your life, vision loss, a seizure,
heavy bleeding, no fetal movement, fainting, or thoughts of self-harm. When unsure, it is
always reasonable to call your provider's office or a nurse line first; they would rather
hear from you early than late.

## How HeartToMom supports you

HeartToMom helps you track blood pressure, heart rate, daily symptoms, and mood, and turns
that into a risk picture you can share with your care team — it supports you, it does not
diagnose or replace your provider. Daily check-ins help spot trends early, like a creeping
blood pressure or new symptoms, so you can raise them at the right time. The app cannot
provide emergency care: for any emergency warning sign, contact your provider or emergency
services directly. Bring your tracked numbers and symptom history to appointments — having
that record helps your provider understand your pattern and make decisions with you.
